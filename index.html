Riccardo Pazzi

PhD student at Bielefeld University, in Barbara Hammer's group. I work on physics-informed neural networks and adversarial robustness for critical infrastructure. Before this I built privacy-preserving machine learning systems on homomorphic encryption.

Research

Learned models are increasingly used to simulate and control physical infrastructure, because solving the governing equations at operational speed is expensive. That substitution introduces two failure modes. A purely data-driven model can return states that violate conservation laws, and it can be pushed far off distribution by small, structured perturbations of its inputs. In systems where the output moves a pump or trips a breaker, both matter.

My PhD sits at that intersection: how physics-informed constraints and adversarial robustness interact in models of water and power networks. Do the constraints buy robustness for free, or do they open new attack surfaces? Where the two objectives conflict, what does the trade-off curve actually look like?

The thread running back through my earlier work is the same one: what guarantees can you make about a learning system beyond its average-case accuracy. That question led me to homomorphic encryption, where the guarantee is confidentiality rather than stability, and where the cost of the guarantee is measured in compute.

I also keep a standing interest in the geometry of representations in large language models, in particular linear probing and the linear representation hypothesis.
